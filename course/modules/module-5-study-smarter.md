# Module 5: Study Smarter (21 min)

*Outcome 5: use AI to study, summarize and learn faster.*

**Module rules to say on camera:** your school's rules come first. Some courses allow AI for studying but not for graded work, and some ban it. Ask your instructor or read the syllabus. AI can be wrong on any subject, so check it against your textbook, lecture and course materials.

---

## Lesson 5.1: Summarize and explain hard material (6 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a dense textbook paragraph on screen, for example about opportunity cost in economics.]

We've all read a paragraph three times and still not understood it. AI is a patient explainer that never gets tired of "wait, can you say that another way?"

There are two different jobs here, and it helps to keep them separate. **Summarizing** makes something shorter. **Explaining** makes something clearer. Both are useful.

[SCREEN: Paste text. Prompt: "Explain this like I'm a first-year student who hasn't taken this course."]

To explain: paste the confusing passage and say who you are. "Explain this in plain English for someone new to the subject. Use one everyday analogy."

[SCREEN: Show the explanation. Ask a follow-up: "I don't get the part about..."]

Then ask follow-ups about the exact part you don't get. That's the power. You can keep asking until it clicks.

[SCREEN: Now paste your lecture notes. Prompt for a summary with structure.]

To summarize your notes: "Summarize these notes into a study guide with headings, key terms with definitions, and three things I'm likely to be tested on."

[SCREEN: Show a mistake: highlight a claim in the AI's explanation that differs from the textbook.]

Now the important part. **Check it against your course material.** AI can oversimplify or get details wrong, especially with newer research, specific formulas or your professor's particular definitions. If the AI's answer disagrees with your textbook or lecture, trust your course materials and ask your instructor.

[SCREEN: Show the "Teach it back" trick.]

Here's a strong learning move. After the explanation, close it and try to explain the idea yourself in your own words. Then paste your explanation and ask: "What did I get right, and what did I miss?" That's active learning, and it works better than just reading.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Reading a summary can feel like learning, but it isn't the same as understanding. Use AI to get unstuck, then test yourself.

**Good to know:** if you upload lecture slides or PDFs, check your school's policy on sharing course materials, and note that upload features vary by tool and plan.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan is confused by "opportunity cost" in Econ 101.

```
I'm a first-year student in Intro Economics. Explain "opportunity cost" in plain English with one everyday analogy and one example from a student's life. Then ask me one question to check that I understood.
```
Jordan answers the check question, gets feedback, then compares the AI's definition with the textbook's.

### Prompt templates
```
Explain [concept] to me as if I'm new to [subject]. Use plain English and one everyday analogy. Then ask me 1 question to check I understood.
```
```
Here are my lecture notes. Turn them into a study guide with: headings, key terms with short definitions, and 3 things likely to be tested. Only use the information in my notes; if something seems unclear or missing, list it at the end.

Notes: [paste]
```
```
Here's my explanation of [concept] in my own words. What did I get right? What did I miss or get wrong? Don't rewrite it; just give feedback.

My explanation: [paste]
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Choose one concept from a current class. Ask for an explanation, then close the chat and write your own in three sentences. Paste it back for feedback, and check the key facts against your textbook.

### Quiz
1. **What's the difference between summarizing and explaining?** Summarizing shortens; explaining makes it clearer.
2. **What should you do if the AI disagrees with your textbook?** Trust your course materials and ask your instructor.
3. **What's the "teach it back" method?** Explain the idea in your own words and ask for feedback.

## Lesson 5.2: Flashcards and practice quizzes (6 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a stack of index cards, then a flashcard app.]

Testing yourself is one of the best ways to remember things. Researchers call it retrieval practice: pulling information out of your head instead of just rereading it. And AI can make practice materials from your own notes in minutes.

[SCREEN: Paste notes. Prompt for flashcards in a table.]

Start with flashcards. Paste your notes and say: "Make 15 flashcards from these notes in a two-column table: term or question, then answer. Only use my notes."

[SCREEN: Show the table. Copy it into a spreadsheet or flashcard app.]

You can copy the table into a spreadsheet, or import it into a flashcard app. Many apps accept a simple two-column format.

[SCREEN: Now practice quiz. Prompt: "Quiz me one question at a time."]

Next, quizzes. "Quiz me on these notes, one question at a time. Wait for my answer, tell me if I'm right, and explain why. Mix multiple-choice and short answer."

[SCREEN: Answer a question wrong on purpose. Show the feedback.]

This is great for the night before a test. You get immediate feedback, and it can revisit what you missed.

[SCREEN: Highlight a flashcard with a wrong answer.]

Now, quality control. AI-generated flashcards can contain mistakes. So review each card before you rely on it. Are the definitions correct? Are they from your class or generic? If the card says something that isn't in your notes, check it or delete it.

[SCREEN: Show a "harder questions" prompt.]

If the questions are too easy, ask: "Make the questions harder and test application, not just definitions." If they're too hard: "Start with easier ones."

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Do not memorize the AI's practice questions and assume they match the real test. They're practice, not a leak of your exam. Use them to find gaps in your knowledge, then go back to your course material to fix them.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Maya has a Financial Accounting exam and a page of notes on the accounting equation and adjusting entries (Assets = Liabilities + Equity).

```
Using ONLY the notes below, make 12 flashcards as a two-column table (Question | Answer). Then write 5 practice questions that test application, with an answer key at the end. If something in my notes is unclear or seems wrong, list it separately.

Notes: [paste]
```
Maya checks the answer key against her textbook problems before studying from it.

### Prompt templates
```
Using ONLY these notes, make [15] flashcards as a table with two columns: Question | Answer. Keep answers under 20 words. After the table, list anything in the notes that seems unclear or possibly incorrect.

Notes: [paste]
```
```
Quiz me on these notes, one question at a time. Mix multiple-choice and short answer. Wait for my answer, tell me if I'm right, explain why, then ask the next one. After 10 questions, summarize my weak spots.

Notes: [paste]
```
```
Make the questions harder, focusing on applying ideas to new situations rather than definitions.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Make 10 flashcards from your notes, check each against the source, and run a 5-question quiz. Write down your two weakest topics.

### Quiz
1. **What is retrieval practice?** Practicing pulling information out of memory, like with flashcards and quizzes.
2. **Why review AI-made flashcards?** They can contain errors or content not in your class.
3. **Should you assume the practice quiz matches the real exam?** No. Use it to find gaps.

## Lesson 5.3: Build a realistic study plan (5 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a calendar with three exam dates, an assignment due date and a work shift.]

Feeling overwhelmed usually isn't about how much you have to do. It's about not knowing where to start. A study plan turns a pile of stress into a list of steps.

[SCREEN: Open a notes doc. List information: exams, due dates, work hours, weekly free hours.]

Step one: gather the facts. Exam dates, assignment deadlines, how many hours you can realistically study each day, your work or practice schedule, and which topics you're weakest in.

[SCREEN: Type the prompt with all the details. Highlight "realistic" and "include breaks."]

Step two: prompt. "Create a two-week study plan. Here's my schedule, my exams and my weak spots. Be realistic. Include breaks, and keep no more than three hours of studying per weekday."

[SCREEN: Show the plan as a table. Paste it into a calendar.]

You get a day-by-day plan. Copy it into your calendar or planner. A plan in a chat you'll never open doesn't help.

[SCREEN: Show the plan not fitting reality. Type an adjustment.]

Step three: adjust. If the plan is too packed, tell it. "Reduce Tuesday, I have work until 8." If you fall behind, ask it to re-plan: "I missed Wednesday. Re-plan the rest."

[SCREEN: Show a study technique list: spaced repetition, active recall, practice problems.]

You can also ask it to build in proven methods: spread topics out over several days, mix in quizzes, and finish each session with a quick recall test instead of rereading.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

One caution. The AI doesn't know how long things actually take you. Treat the plan as a first draft, and adjust after the first two days. Also, if you feel constantly overwhelmed or anxious, talk to someone at your school's advising or counseling center. A plan can help with workload, but it isn't a replacement for support.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan has an Econ midterm in 8 days, a lab report due in 5 days, works Tuesday/Thursday evenings, and can study about 2 hours on weeknights and 4 on Saturday. Weak topics: elasticity, supply shifts.

```
Create an 8-day study plan starting tomorrow. Constraints: I work Tuesday and Thursday 4–9 p.m.; I can study 2 hours on other weeknights and 4 hours on Saturday; Sunday is off. Deadlines: Lab report due on day 5; Econ midterm on day 8. Weak topics: elasticity and supply shifts (give them more time). Include short breaks, one practice-quiz session per topic, and a light final day. Present as a table: Day | Time | Task.
```

### Prompt templates
```
Create a [number]-day study plan starting [date]. My deadlines: [list]. My fixed commitments: [work, classes, practice]. Available study time: [hours]. Weak topics: [list]. Be realistic, include breaks, active recall and practice questions, and present it as a table: Day | Task | Time.
```
```
I fell behind. Here's what I finished: [list]. Here's the time I have left: [details]. Re-plan the remaining days, prioritizing [exam/assignment].
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Gather your next two weeks of deadlines. Generate a plan, adjust one day so it's realistic, and put it in your calendar.

### Quiz
1. **What information should you give for a study plan?** Deadlines, fixed commitments, available time and weak topics.
2. **Why put the plan in your calendar?** So you'll actually see and use it.
3. **What should you do when you fall behind?** Tell the AI what you finished and ask it to re-plan.

## Lesson 5.4: Learn with AI, not instead of learning (4 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera.]

Now the honest part. AI can make school easier in two ways: it can help you understand, or it can help you skip the understanding. The second one feels easier, but it costs you later, on the exam, in interviews and at work.

[SCREEN: Show a two-column slide: "Learning with AI" and "Skipping the learning."]

Learning with AI looks like this: asking it to explain something you're stuck on, quizzing yourself, getting feedback on your own draft, and making a study plan. In each case, you're doing the thinking.

Skipping looks like this: having it write the essay you were assigned, solve the homework, or answer the discussion post, then submitting it as yours.

[SCREEN: Show a sample syllabus AI policy.]

**Check your school's rules.** Read the syllabus. Some instructors allow AI for brainstorming but not for writing. Some require you to disclose it. Some ban it. If it's unclear, ask. Something like: "May I use AI to check my grammar or explain concepts, as long as the work is my own?" That question is professional, and most instructors respect it.

[SCREEN: Show the consequences: "Academic integrity violation."]

Breaking those rules can lead to academic integrity violations, and AI detection tools are unreliable, so relying on "they can't tell" isn't a plan. It's a risk. Also, "the AI did it" isn't a defense.

[SCREEN: Show a 3-question check: Is it allowed? Is it my thinking? Could I explain it without the AI?]

Here's a quick test. One: is this allowed in this class? Two: is the thinking mine? Three: could I explain this work to my professor without AI open? If any answer is no, stop and rethink.

[SCREEN: Show examples of good uses: outline feedback, grammar check.]

Good uses: ask for feedback on your own thesis statement, check your reasoning on a practice problem after you've tried it, and get a simpler explanation of a reading.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Use AI as a tutor, not a ghostwriter. You're paying for an education. The skills are the point.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Maya's professor allows AI "for studying but not for writing graded work."

- Allowed: ask AI to explain a concept, quiz her, give feedback on her outline **if** she asks, and check grammar (confirm with the professor).
- Not allowed: generate paragraphs of the graded essay.

Maya emails: "Is it okay if I use AI to quiz myself and to explain readings, provided all submitted writing is my own?"

### Prompt templates
```
I've written this thesis statement for my essay: "[paste]". Don't rewrite it. Ask me 3 questions that would help me make it clearer and more specific, and point out any weakness in my reasoning.
```
```
I tried this problem and got [answer]. Here's my work: [paste]. Don't give me the solution yet. Give me a hint about where my reasoning might have gone wrong.
```
```
Act as a tutor. I'm going to explain [concept] and you tell me what I missed. Don't give me the full answer until I ask.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Read the AI policy in one of your syllabi (or ask your instructor). Write a one-sentence rule for how you'll use AI in that class.

### Quiz
1. **What are the three self-check questions?** Is it allowed? Is the thinking mine? Could I explain it without AI?
2. **Are AI detectors a safe reason to rely on "they can't tell"?** No. They're unreliable, and the work would still break the rules.
3. **Give one good use of AI for studying.** Any of: explaining concepts, quizzing, feedback on your own outline, study plans.
