# Module 6: Side Projects and Small Business (22 min)

*Outcome 6: use AI to plan and execute a side project or small business.*

**Module rules to say on camera:** AI can help you think and draft. It can't predict whether an idea will make money, and no plan guarantees results. Never rely on it for legal, tax or licensing advice. Check local rules and ask a professional when it matters. Don't paste customer data.

---

## Lesson 6.1: Brainstorm and pressure-test an idea (6 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a notes app titled "Side project ideas" with three vague ideas.]

Maybe you've got an idea for a side project. Or maybe you have no idea, and you just know you'd like to build something. AI is a great brainstorming partner, as long as you use it for more than just agreeing with you.

[SCREEN: Type a brainstorming prompt with your skills, interests, time and budget.]

Start with **you**. Give it your skills, your interests, how many hours a week you have, and how much money you can spend, which might be zero. Ask for ten ideas that fit those limits.

[SCREEN: Show the list. Circle two.]

You'll get a list. Some will be generic. Circle the two or three you'd actually enjoy working on for a few months.

[SCREEN: Type the "devil's advocate" prompt.]

Now the step most beginners skip: **pressure-test it.** AI tends to be encouraging, so tell it to be critical. "Act as a skeptical friend. Give me the five biggest reasons this idea might not work and what I'd need to check."

[SCREEN: Show the answer. Highlight: "Who is the customer?" "Why would they pay?" "Who else does this?"]

Here's what it asks. Who's the customer? Why would they pay? Who else does this already? How would they find me? Those are the right questions.

[SCREEN: Show a note listing "Things to check, not assume."]

Then, and this is key, the AI can raise questions but it can't answer them. It doesn't know your local market or what people will pay. So turn its answers into a to-do list of things to check. Talk to five people who might be your customers. Look at competitors. Search for similar products and their prices.

[SCREEN: Show a warning slide: "AI can't validate demand."]

AI can't validate demand. Only real people can. And if it gives you market-size numbers or statistics, treat those as unverified until you find the source.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

By the end of this lesson, you want one idea and a list of three things to check this week.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan likes design and has about 5 hours a week and $50.

```
I'm a college student with 5 hours a week and $50 to start. My skills: graphic design in Canva, running a club Instagram, and writing. My interests: sustainability and stickers. Give me 10 side-project ideas that fit these limits. For each, add one sentence on who the customer would be.
```
Then:
```
Act as a skeptical friend. For idea #4 (custom sticker packs for campus clubs), give me the 5 biggest reasons it could fail, and list what I'd need to verify. Don't invent statistics.
```
Jordan turns the answers into a checklist: ask 5 club presidents, look up printing costs, check what other sellers charge.

### Prompt templates
```
I'm [who]. I have [X hours/week] and [budget]. My skills: [list]. My interests: [list]. Give me 10 side-project ideas that fit those limits. For each, add a one-sentence description of who the customer would be.
```
```
Act as a skeptical, experienced friend. For this idea: [idea]. Give me the 5 biggest reasons it might not work, and for each, what I could check this week. Don't invent statistics or market data.
```
```
Turn those risks into a checklist of 5 concrete, low-cost things I can do this week to test the idea.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Generate ideas, choose one, run the skeptic prompt and write down three things you'll check this week.

### Quiz
1. **Why tell the AI to be critical?** It tends to be encouraging, so you need it to surface risks.
2. **Can AI tell you whether people will buy?** No. Only real customer feedback can.
3. **What should you do with statistics the AI gives you?** Treat them as unverified and find the source.

## Lesson 6.2: Turn the idea into a 30-day plan (5 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a big blank whiteboard.]

Ideas are easy. Starting is hard, because a big idea feels like a big pile of work. A plan makes it smaller. And the smallest useful plan is a 30-day test.

[SCREEN: Type the prompt: "Create a 30-day plan to test this idea with minimal money."]

Give the AI your idea, your weekly hours and your budget. And here's the important part: ask for a **test**, not a launch. "What's the smallest, cheapest way to find out whether anyone wants this?"

[SCREEN: Show the plan in weeks: Week 1 research, Week 2 build a simple version, Week 3 share it, Week 4 review.]

You get a week-by-week plan. Week one: research and talk to people. Week two: make a simple version. Week three: show it to real people. Week four: review what happened and decide what to do next.

[SCREEN: Show the plan being trimmed to fit 5 hours a week.]

Then be honest about time. If the plan says 15 hours a week and you have 5, say so: "Cut this to fit 5 hours a week. Prioritize the tasks that test demand."

[SCREEN: Paste tasks into a to-do list or calendar.]

Move the tasks into a calendar or to-do app with dates. And define what success looks like *before* you start: "If at least five people say they'd pay, I'll continue." That stops you from moving the goalposts.

[SCREEN: Show a review prompt.]

At the end of 30 days, paste in your notes and ask: "Here's what happened. What did I learn, and what are my next three options?" The AI can help you think. The decision is yours.

[SCREEN: Show a legal caution.]

Finally, if you sell something, there may be rules: business registration, taxes, licenses, platform policies. AI can give general information, but it can be wrong or out of date. Check official sources or ask a professional.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

One idea, one plan, thirty days. That's enough.

### Worked example
```
Idea: custom sticker packs for campus clubs. I have 5 hours a week and $50. Create a 30-day plan to test whether clubs will pay. Week by week, include specific tasks under 1 hour each, one success measure I can check at the end, and things to research about rules for selling on campus. Don't assume anything about local rules; list them as questions to check.
```

### Prompt templates
```
Idea: [idea]. I have [hours/week] and [budget]. Create a 30-day plan to test whether people want this, not to fully launch. Break it into weeks with tasks under [X] minutes each. Include one clear success measure, and list any rules or legal questions I should check with official sources.
```
```
This plan takes more time than I have. Cut it to fit [hours/week], keeping the tasks that best test demand.
```
```
Here's what happened during my 30-day test: [notes]. What did I learn, and what are 3 options for next steps? List what I still don't know.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Generate a plan for your idea. Cut it to fit your real time, and put the first week's tasks in your calendar.

### Quiz
1. **What should a 30-day plan aim to do?** Test whether people want the idea, cheaply.
2. **Why define success beforehand?** So you can judge results honestly and not move the goalposts.
3. **Should you rely on AI for legal or tax rules?** No. Check official sources or ask a professional.

## Lesson 6.3: Marketing copy and content (6 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then an online shop page with an empty product description.]

You've got something to sell or share. Now you need words: a product description, a bio, captions, a simple announcement. Writing about yourself is awkward, and AI can help you get past the blank page.

[SCREEN: Open your voice note.]

First, bring your voice note from Module One. Marketing copy is where robotic writing shows up the most: "Elevate your experience with our premium, must-have..." Nobody talks like that.

[SCREEN: Type a product description prompt. Highlight the real details.]

Give it real details: what it is, who it's for, what makes it different, price and how to get it. And say "use only these details." The AI loves to invent features, like "eco-friendly materials" or "handmade," so don't let it claim anything that isn't true.

[SCREEN: Show three descriptions. Pick one and edit.]

Ask for three versions in different tones and choose one.

[SCREEN: Type a content prompt: "Give me 10 post ideas."]

For content: "Give me ten post ideas for [platform] for [audience], based on [what I sell]." Pick the ones you can actually make. A plan you can keep up beats a perfect plan you drop.

[SCREEN: Show a checklist: claims, prices, dates, disclosures.]

Before you post, check the claims. Is every statement true? Are prices and dates right? If you say "best" or "guaranteed" or make health, income or environmental claims, you need proof, and some claims have legal rules. Keep it factual and modest.

[SCREEN: Show disclosure examples.]

Also, follow platform rules and disclosure requirements, like labeling sponsored content. And don't use AI to fake reviews or testimonials. That's deceptive, and it's against most platforms' rules.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Use AI for drafts. Add your own photos, your own stories and your own voice. That's what people connect with.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan sells recycled-paper sticker packs.

```
Write 3 short product descriptions (under 50 words each) for a sticker pack. Use ONLY these facts: 6 stickers per pack; designed by me; printed on paper made with 30% recycled content (per the printer's spec sheet); $6; sold at campus events and via DM. Tone: friendly, plain-spoken. Style: [voice note]. Don't add claims about durability, materials or impact beyond what I've listed.
```

### Prompt templates
```
Write 3 versions of a product description (under [X] words). Use ONLY these facts: [list]. Audience: [who]. Tone: [tone]. Style: [voice note]. Don't add features, claims, or statistics that aren't listed.
```
```
Give me 10 post ideas for [platform] for [audience], based on [what I sell/do]. Mix behind-the-scenes, helpful tips and product posts. Only ideas I can create with a phone and 30 minutes.
```
```
Write a short bio (under 40 words) for my [shop/page]. Facts: [list]. Warm and plain. No buzzwords.
```
```
List every claim in this copy that would need proof or might have legal rules.

Copy: [paste]
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Write three true facts about your project. Generate one description and five post ideas. Highlight every claim and check it.

### Quiz
1. **Why say "use only these facts"?** So the AI doesn't invent product claims.
2. **Is it okay to generate fake reviews?** No. It's deceptive and against most platform rules.
3. **What should you check before posting?** Claims, prices, dates and disclosure rules.

## Lesson 6.4: Customer messages and simple operations (5 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a phone with a DM inbox showing three repeat questions.]

Once people start asking questions, you'll answer the same ones over and over. "How much is it?" "When will it ship?" "Can I return it?" Templates save time, and AI helps you write them.

[SCREEN: Open a document titled "Customer replies."]

First, collect your real questions. Even if you only have three so far. Write your real answers: price, shipping time, return policy, contact.

[SCREEN: Type the prompt with policies and ask for templates.]

Then ask: "Turn these into short, friendly reply templates. Use my style. Use [BRACKETS] for anything that changes, like name and date." Save them.

[SCREEN: Show an FAQ section for a shop page.]

The same material becomes an FAQ page. "Turn these into an FAQ with five questions and short answers."

[SCREEN: Show a difficult message: a complaint.]

For complaints, get help with tone but keep the decision human. "A customer says their order arrived damaged. Draft a reply that apologizes, asks for a photo, and offers [the fix I've decided]." Don't let the AI promise refunds or make policy you didn't choose.

[SCREEN: Show a privacy warning.]

**Privacy.** Don't paste customer names, addresses or order details into AI. Use placeholders. Customers trust you with their information.

[SCREEN: Show simple operations: order tracker, weekly checklist.]

You can also ask AI to design a simple order tracker (that's Module Three), a weekly checklist, or a packing routine. Keep it light.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Remember: templates should sound like a person. Read each one and ask, would I be happy receiving this? If not, make it warmer.

### Worked example
```
I sell sticker packs. Turn my policies into 4 short, friendly reply templates in my style [voice note]: (1) price question, (2) shipping time, (3) damaged item, (4) custom order request. Use [BRACKETS] for names and dates. Policies: $6/pack; ships within 3 business days; if damaged, I replace it after a photo; custom orders take 2 weeks and need a minimum of 20 packs. Don't add policies I haven't listed.
```

### Prompt templates
```
Turn these policies into [number] short, friendly reply templates for common customer questions. Use my style: [voice note]. Use [BRACKETS] for anything that changes. Don't add policies I haven't listed.

Policies: [paste]
```
```
A customer says: "[paste with personal details removed]". Draft a reply that acknowledges the problem, asks for [what I need], and offers [the decision I've made]. Under 80 words. Don't promise anything I haven't listed.
```
```
Turn these into an FAQ with [5] questions and short answers: [paste]
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Write down your top three customer (or future customer) questions, then generate templates and save them in your prompt library.

### Quiz
1. **What should you use for details that change?** [BRACKETS] as placeholders.
2. **Who decides refund and return policies?** You, not the AI.
3. **Should you paste customers' personal details into AI?** No. Use placeholders.
