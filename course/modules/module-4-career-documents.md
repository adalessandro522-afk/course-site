# Module 4: Resume, Cover Letter and LinkedIn (25 min)

*Outcome 4: create a strong resume, cover letter and LinkedIn profile with AI help.*

**Module rules to say on camera:** everything on your resume must be true and something you can talk about in an interview. AI helps you word your real experience. It doesn't create experience. Don't paste anyone else's personal details, and remove your own phone number, home address and similar details before pasting.

---

## Lesson 4.1: Turn real experience into strong resume bullets (7 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a resume with weak bullets highlighted: "Responsible for social media," "Helped with customers," "Worked on projects."]

Look at these bullets. "Responsible for social media." "Helped with customers." They're true. They're also forgettable, because they say what you were assigned, not what you did or what happened.

Strong bullets are specific: what you did, how, and the result, ideally with a number.

[SCREEN: Show a formula slide: "Action verb + what you did + how/tool + result."]

Here's a simple structure. Start with an action verb. Say what you did. Mention the tool or method. Then the result, if you know it.

But the AI can't know your results. If you ask it to "write a strong bullet about my social media job," it will invent numbers. "Increased engagement by 300%." Don't use that. That's fabrication, and interviewers will ask about it.

[SCREEN: Open a document called "Experience Dump." Show raw notes.]

So the first step is on you. Before you touch AI, write a messy dump about each experience. What did you do? What tools did you use? What went well? Any numbers you actually know: how many posts, how many people, how much time, how much money?

[SCREEN: Show Antonio's own example of a raw dump for an internship, generic and fictional: "Reconciled bank statements in QuickBooks for small business clients. Found and fixed discrepancies. Did journal entries during month-end close."]

Here's mine, in plain language. Now I give this to the AI with clear rules.

[SCREEN: Type the prompt. Highlight "use only the facts I've given," and "don't add numbers."]

The key rules: use only the facts I gave you, don't add numbers or tools I didn't mention, and give me three versions of each bullet. If it needs a number, it should ask me.

[SCREEN: Show output bullets. Then edit them.]

I get stronger bullets. And I check each one: is it true? Could I explain it in an interview? Did it exaggerate my role? "Led" when I only "helped" is a problem. I fix the words to match reality.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

And if the AI asks "how many?" and you don't know, it's fine to leave the number out, or estimate honestly, like "about 20 accounts." Only use numbers you can defend.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Maya's raw dump for a campus job at a coffee shop.
> Worked front counter about 15 hrs/week. Trained two new hires on the register. Suggested a sign for the seasonal menu and the manager put it up. Handled cash at close, counted the drawer.

```
Turn my raw notes into 3 resume bullets for a [target role: marketing coordinator] application. Rules: use only the facts in my notes; do not add numbers, tools or results I haven't given; start each with a strong action verb; keep each under 25 words; if a number would help, ask me a question instead of guessing.

Notes: [paste]
```
Sample results: "Trained two new team members on point-of-sale procedures." "Proposed a seasonal-menu display that management adopted." Maya keeps "about 15 hours a week" only because it's true.

### Prompt templates
**Bullet builder**
```
Turn these notes into [3] resume bullets for [target role]. Rules: use only the facts below; don't invent numbers, tools, results or titles; start each bullet with a strong action verb; max 25 words each; if you need more information, ask me questions first.

Notes: [paste your raw experience dump]
```
**Truth check**
```
Compare these resume bullets with my original notes. List anything in the bullets that isn't supported by the notes.

Notes: [paste]
Bullets: [paste]
```
**Action verbs without buzzwords**
```
Suggest 10 specific action verbs I could use for these tasks, avoiding vague phrases like "responsible for" and "helped with": [tasks]
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Write a raw dump for one experience. Generate bullets with the builder prompt, run the truth check, and keep only the bullets you can defend.

### Quiz
1. **Why shouldn't you ask AI to "make my job sound impressive"?** It may invent facts or numbers you can't back up.
2. **What should you prepare before prompting?** A raw dump of what you actually did, tools used, and real numbers.
3. **What's the "truth check"?** Compare the bullets to your notes and remove anything unsupported.

## Lesson 4.2: Tailor your resume to a job posting (5 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a job posting with key phrases highlighted.]

Sending the exact same resume to every job usually doesn't work well. Many employers use software that scans for keywords, and humans skim for about thirty seconds. You want the most relevant things to be easy to find.

Tailoring means reordering and rewording what's true, not adding what isn't.

[SCREEN: Paste the job posting into the chat.]

Step one: paste the job posting and ask the AI to pull out the top skills, keywords and responsibilities. "List the 8 most important requirements. Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves."

[SCREEN: Show the list.]

Now I know what they care about. Maybe it's "customer communication," "Excel," "attention to detail."

[SCREEN: Paste in your resume bullets. Prompt: "Compare my resume to this job posting."]

Step two: paste your resume, with contact details removed, and ask for a comparison. "Which of my existing bullets match these requirements? Which requirements aren't covered? Don't suggest adding anything untrue."

[SCREEN: Show the gap analysis.]

This is really helpful, because it shows where you're strong and where you have a gap. A gap isn't a disaster. Maybe you have the experience but didn't mention it. Maybe you honestly don't have it yet.

[SCREEN: Ask for rewording suggestions, using the posting's language where true.]

Step three: rewording. "Suggest how to reword my existing bullets using the posting's language, only where it accurately describes what I did."

[SCREEN: Show keyword stuffing being rejected: a red X over a list of copied buzzwords.]

Don't keyword-stuff. Copy-pasting a list of skills you don't have isn't clever. It'll come up in the interview, and it can cost you the job.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Keep a master resume with everything, and make a tailored one-page version for each application. AI makes that quick. Just read every line before you submit.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan applies for a "Marketing Assistant" role. The posting stresses social media scheduling, Canva and reporting. Jordan's existing bullets mention running a club Instagram, designing flyers in Canva and tracking sign-ups. AI maps those to the requirements and flags one gap (no email marketing experience). Jordan doesn't add it. Jordan mentions a relevant class project instead, only if true.

### Prompt templates
```
Here's a job posting. List the 8 most important requirements, separating must-haves from nice-to-haves, and list the keywords that appear repeatedly.

Posting: [paste]
```
```
Compare my resume bullets to the requirements you just listed. Tell me: (1) which bullets match, (2) which requirements aren't covered, (3) how to reword existing bullets using the posting's language ONLY where it accurately describes what I did. Do not suggest adding experience I don't have.

Resume: [paste, without contact info]
```
```
Check my tailored bullets against my original resume. Flag anything that appears to be new or exaggerated.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Find one real job posting. Extract the requirements, run the comparison against your resume and update two bullets truthfully.

### Quiz
1. **What does tailoring mean?** Emphasizing and rewording true experience to match the job.
2. **What's wrong with keyword stuffing?** You may claim skills you don't have, which can come out in interviews.
3. **What should you remove before pasting your resume?** Personal contact details.

## Lesson 4.3: Cover letters that don't sound generic (6 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a generic cover letter: "I am writing to express my interest in the position at your esteemed company."]

Hiring managers read a lot of cover letters that all sound like that. "I am writing to express my interest." "Passionate team player." AI can produce generic cover letters instantly, which is exactly why yours has to be specific.

A good cover letter is short, about three or four paragraphs, and answers three questions. Why this role? Why this company? Why you, based on evidence?

[SCREEN: Show a note with three headings and bullet points beneath: "Why this company," "Two relevant things I've done," "What I want to do."]

Step one: you write the ingredients. Not the letter, just notes. One real reason you're interested in this company. Two experiences or projects that fit the role. What you hope to do.

The "real reason" is what makes it human. Something you read, used, or saw. If you can't think of one, do a little research first.

[SCREEN: Paste notes, job posting summary and voice note into the prompt.]

Step two: give the AI the ingredients: the job posting, your notes, your voice note, and rules. "Under 250 words. No clichés. Don't claim anything I haven't told you. No 'I am writing to express my interest.'"

[SCREEN: Show the draft. Highlight one sentence that's too polished.]

Step three: edit. Read it out loud. Find the sentence that's too polished and make it plainer. Check every claim and the company name. Wrong company name in a cover letter is more common than you'd think.

[SCREEN: Show a checklist.]

Checklist. Correct company and role name. One specific reason for this company. Every claim is true. Sounds like me. Under one page.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

And in some cases, applicants are asked whether they used AI. Follow the instructions of the application. Being honest about using AI as a drafting tool is better than hiding it. But the ideas and experiences must be yours.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Maya applies to a small skincare company as a marketing coordinator.

Maya's ingredients: bought the brand's product after seeing its ingredient-transparency posts; ran her sorority's Instagram (grew posting consistency to 4 posts a week, her true number); made a customer survey for a class project.

```
Write a cover letter under 250 words for a Marketing Coordinator role at [Company]. Use only my notes. Don't add achievements, numbers or claims I haven't listed. No clichés such as "I am writing to express my interest" or "passionate team player." Use my style: [voice note].

Job posting summary: [paste]
My notes:
- Why this company: [note]
- Experience 1: [note]
- Experience 2: [note]
- What I want to do: [note]
```

### Prompt templates
```
Write a cover letter (max 250 words) for [role] at [company]. Use only the notes below. Do not invent achievements, numbers or company facts. Avoid clichés. Tone: [tone]. Style: [voice note]. End with a simple, confident closing.

Job posting summary: [paste]
Notes: [paste]
```
```
Read this cover letter as a hiring manager with 30 seconds. What stands out? What sounds generic? Which sentences could be cut? Don't rewrite the whole thing.
```
```
List every factual claim in this cover letter so I can verify each one.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Write your three ingredient notes for a real (or pretend) role. Generate a draft, cut 20%, and read it out loud.

### Quiz
1. **What three questions should a cover letter answer?** Why this role, why this company, why you (with evidence).
2. **What makes a cover letter feel human?** A specific, true reason and your own experiences and voice.
3. **What should you verify at the end?** Company name, role, and every claim.

## Lesson 4.4: Your LinkedIn profile (4 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera. Then a LinkedIn profile page with a sample profile, default headline "Student at University."]

LinkedIn is like a living resume that recruiters search. Three parts matter most for a beginner: the headline, the About section, and your experience entries.

[SCREEN: Highlight the headline.]

**Headline.** By default it shows your job title or school. You can use the space to say who you are and what you're aiming for. Ask AI for five options: "Based on these notes, give me five headline options under 120 characters. No buzzwords."

[SCREEN: Show options. Pick one.]

Pick the one that's clear and true.

[SCREEN: Highlight the About section.]

**About.** This is a short summary in first person. Three to five sentences: what you study or do, what you're interested in, and what you're looking for. Give the AI your notes and your voice, and ask for a draft in first person.

[SCREEN: Show a draft. Cross out "results-driven professional with a proven track record."]

Cut the stock phrases: "results-driven," "passionate," "proven track record." They say nothing. Replace them with specifics.

[SCREEN: Show experience entries, paste in resume bullets.]

**Experience.** Reuse your resume bullets, and you can make them slightly more conversational. Ask AI to adapt them without adding facts.

[SCREEN: Show a privacy check.]

**Privacy check.** Don't put your phone number or home address in public sections. Check what's visible to non-connections. And don't paste your whole profile with private details into AI.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Then ask a friend or mentor to read it. Everything on the profile should match what's on your resume, so check they agree.

### Worked example
**Scenario:** Jordan, a junior studying environmental science.

Headline options might include: "Environmental Science Student | Interested in Sustainability Consulting" or "Junior at [University] | Data, Sustainability and Community Projects." Jordan picks the first because it's plain and true.

### Prompt templates
```
Give me 5 LinkedIn headline options under 120 characters. Use only these facts: [major, school, interests, target industry]. No buzzwords such as "passionate" or "results-driven."
```
```
Write a first-person LinkedIn "About" section of 4 sentences. Use only these facts: [notes]. Style: [voice note]. No clichés. End with what I'm looking for: [goal].
```
```
Adapt these resume bullets for a LinkedIn experience entry. Keep them accurate, slightly more conversational, and don't add facts.
Bullets: [paste]
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Write a new headline and About section. Compare with your resume for consistency, and remove anything you can't back up.

### Quiz
1. **Which three sections matter most for a beginner?** Headline, About, and experience entries.
2. **What should you avoid in the About section?** Empty buzzwords and unverified claims.
3. **What should you check about privacy?** Don't post phone or address publicly, and check what non-connections can see.

## Lesson 4.5: Interview practice partner (3 min)

### Script

[SCREEN: You on camera.]

Practicing interviews out loud is one of the best uses of AI, because it costs nothing, it's available at 11 p.m., and it doesn't judge you.

[SCREEN: Type the mock interview prompt.]

Here's the setup. Tell it the job, tell it to act as the interviewer, and ask it to ask one question at a time. Say "wait for my answer before continuing."

[SCREEN: Type an answer. Show the feedback.]

I answer. Then I ask: "Give feedback on my answer: what worked, what was unclear, and how I could make it more specific. Under three sentences."

[SCREEN: Show the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result.]

For behavioral questions like "Tell me about a time you faced a challenge," use STAR: the situation, the task, what you did, and the result. Ask the AI to check if your answer follows it.

[SCREEN: On camera.]

Remember, the AI is guessing what interviewers might ask. It's practice, not a prediction. And if you can, say your answers out loud. Speaking is different from typing.

### Worked example
```
Act as an interviewer for an entry-level marketing coordinator role at a small skincare company. Ask me one behavioral question at a time. After I answer, give feedback in 3 sentences or fewer on clarity, specificity and whether I used the STAR structure. Then ask the next question. Start now.
```

### Prompt templates
```
Act as a friendly but realistic interviewer for [role] at [type of company]. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. After each answer, give brief feedback (max 3 sentences) and a suggestion to improve. Do 5 questions.
```
```
Here's my answer to "[question]". Did I use the STAR structure? What's missing? Suggest one way to make it more specific, without adding facts I didn't give.
```

### Do it in 10 minutes
Run a five-question mock interview. Pick your weakest answer and rewrite it, then say it out loud once.

### Quiz
1. **What does STAR stand for?** Situation, Task, Action, Result.
2. **Why tell the AI to ask one question at a time?** It makes it feel like a real interview and lets you respond to each.
3. **Is AI a prediction of the real interview?** No. It's practice.
